# Write learning notes

The default is a readable explanation of the method, not a timestamp digest or a complete course. The reader should understand the video's concrete problem, necessary concepts, causal relationships, the creator's example, and where the explanation applies. Follow the evidence/coverage rules in [Distillation](distillation.md).

Start with a concrete task from the video: its inputs, important action, observable result, and why it matters. Organize around complete questions, such as why a step is needed or how one component affects another. At each transition, use the preceding result, name what it still cannot explain, and show why the new step resolves that difficulty. A transition sentence alone is not knowledge transfer.

Explain unfamiliar terms near their first use and point to what they mean in the running example. Preserve the creator's original example and track intermediate states of the same data, code, diagram, or project. Label invented analogies/examples as illustrative additions, not video content.

Build causal understanding rather than a glossary or checklist: example/observation, mechanism, then relevant terms, formulas and conditions. Put essential prerequisites and reasoning in the main text; reserve optional depth for later. Do not impose a fixed number of sections, exercises, or decorative elements.

Explain the choice as well as the action. For example, a PDF can preserve a mind map's visual arrangement while Markdown provides editable text and links. Explain those two needs and their tradeoff before listing export clicks. If the reader only needs editable text, say why the extra visual copy may be unnecessary. This is a teaching explanation, not evidence that a particular creator used those formats; attribute the example only when supported by that source.

When both deliverables are requested, notes explain why the method works and its boundaries; the companion workflow explains how inputs become outputs. Share the original example and source pointers, but do not repeat every step or make one file unintelligible without the other.

Do not invent missing settings, commands, versions, or actions. Distinguish source content, explanation, inference and verification. Make limitations specific: stated applicability, source date, and claims narrated without demonstration. End by returning to the original task: what can now be understood or attempted, what remains unresolved, and what evidence is missing.

A note commonly connects the core question, the running example, necessary concepts, intermediate changes, key visuals actually inspected, conditions, and remaining gaps. It need not give each a separate heading. Reply in the requested language, otherwise the conversation language; preserve exact source commands/identifiers.

Choose prose, an inspected screenshot, or a clearly labeled teaching diagram for the actual difficulty. Use screenshots for relationships, changes, or results that matter, with a caption explaining what to look at and a clickable source time. A teaching diagram is not an original video frame. Follow [visual inspection](transcription-visual.md#inspect-the-visuals-that-matter).

Keep the main explanation natural. State shared source/coverage limits briefly once; qualify a specific claim where its uncertainty matters. Put test logs and detailed verification in a separate report when needed. Do not erase real gaps to make the prose smoother.

Offer a specific deeper-tutorial topic when useful; expand it only on request. Create a webpage/interactive demonstration only if requested and verify it if created. Reading notes is not proof of mastery. Deep tutoring, exercises, grading, revision schedules and learning-state tracking are separate tasks. A handoff can include source identity, paths, timestamps, coverage, original example and gaps. A few requested self-check questions may adapt the video's example with provenance; do not automatically research a separate question bank.
